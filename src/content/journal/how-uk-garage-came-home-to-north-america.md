Back when Craig David was first getting played on North American radio, a Toronto alt-weekly profiled a DJ and producer named Andy Lewis and his love of UK garage, the shuffling, bass-heavy sound then filling London's clubs and charts. The piece noted that the genre had yet to make much impact in North America. Lewis described introducing himself to American producers at Miami's Winter Music Conference and being teased for it: ["So you're the one making all that funny sped-up garage."](https://nowtoronto.com/music/andy-lewis-locked-on-to-uk-garage/)

Now look at one week in Vancouver. On Halloween, Foundation takes over the [Vancouver Convention Centre](https://www.ticketmaster.ca/mph-tickets/artist/3103241) with Chris Lake, MPH, Benny Benassi and Sam Divine. Six nights later, Sammy Virji brings his North American tour to the [PNE Forum](https://songkick.com/concerts/43322838-sammy-virji-at-pne-forum), the night before Seattle and after earlier stops at [Montreal's Parc Jean-Drapeau and Red Rocks Amphitheatre](https://seatgeek.ca/sammy-virji-tickets/vancouver). Two of the biggest names in the new wave of UK garage are playing rooms that size in the same city inside a week, one of them alongside a [Grammy-nominated](https://audiojacked.com/music-news/chris-lake-atrip-make-you-fight-black-book-records) house producer.

Somewhere between Miami and Vancouver, the funny sped-up garage got big enough to fill those rooms. The odd part is that it was North American to begin with.

## A sound named after a New York club

"Garage" was never a British word for music. It comes from the [Paradise Garage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garage_house), the New York club where Larry Levan held his residency, and from Club Zanzibar in Newark, New Jersey. The soulful, gospel-touched house that DJs played in those rooms became known as garage house, or simply the New York and New Jersey sound. (We wrote about the people who built the Garage's room, including sound designer Richard Long, in [our piece on the designers behind legendary clubs](/journal/the-designers-behind-legendary-clubs/).)

By the early 1990s, that American sound had a devoted following in London, but it lived in odd places. According to [Wikipedia's history of UK garage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UK_garage), US and US-influenced house and garage was very often played in the second room at jungle events, the overflow space for people who wanted something warmer than the main room's breakbeats.

## London sped it up

What happened next was partly a matter of tempo. American garage ran at around 120 beats per minute. London's jungle crowd was used to music far faster, so DJs began pushing American records up to around 130, and the media started calling the result speed garage. Wikipedia's history points to the American producer Todd Edwards' "The Praise (God in His Hand)" as an early example of a garage record played at that faster tempo.

Edwards matters for another reason. His habit of [chopping vocals into tiny fragments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UK_garage) and rearranging them into new rhythms became one of the signatures of the British sound. So even the most British-sounding part of UK garage, the stuttering cut-up vocal, owes a debt to an American producer. Keep his name in mind; it comes back.

By the late 1990s, London producers had taken it further. The most radical change came when they dropped the second and fourth kick drums from each bar, leaving the [skipping, syncopated rhythm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2-step_garage) known as 2-step. Pirate radio carried it, and by 1999 2-step records were on Top of the Pops. That was the sound Andy Lewis was championing, and the one North American dance floors mostly ignored.

## Why it didn't cross the first time

The Toronto profile gave an honest reason: many veterans of the North American house scene [saw UK garage as Britain repackaging Black American music](https://nowtoronto.com/music/andy-lewis-locked-on-to-uk-garage/). It's a fair criticism, and worth sitting with. The sound was built on the work of Black and Latino DJs and dancers in New York and New Jersey, and the British version arrived with more chart hits than credit.

So for most of the 2000s, UK garage stayed largely British. Its influence spread in other ways, feeding into grime, dubstep and UK funky, but a North American kid was more likely to hear it second-hand than at a club.

## The second wave

The revival started in the underground. In 2023, [Bandcamp Daily reported](https://daily.bandcamp.com/scene-report/nukg-scene-report) that a new generation of garage, nicknamed NUKG, had been bubbling under for about four years, led by producers such as Interplanetary Criminal, whose explanation for the appeal was simple: "Everyone kind of likes garage, right?"

This time the internet did the job pirate radio once did, and the sound didn't stay in London. The UK garage column NUKG Monthly at KEYMAG has since covered growing scenes in [Japan](https://www.keymag.co.uk/nukg-monthly/inside-japans-garage-scene-prozak-ec2a-pangaea) and Brazil, and in 2026 it profiled [a California scene](https://www.keymag.co.uk/nukg-monthly/ramonpang-interview-uk-garage-from-california) that has released a compilation called *UK Garage from California*. The same column admits British heads are still a little suspicious of the American version, noting that the garage hype cycle has swept through festivals including Coachella and EDC, and joking about the way Americans pronounce the word.

The numbers moved too. Stereofox, citing an [IMS Business Report](https://www.stereofox.com/articles/fastest-rising-electronic-music-genres), reports that plays of the UK garage scene on SoundCloud rose 31% in a year, with the US the second-biggest audience after the UK.

The best-known name in North America is Sammy Virji, the Southampton-born producer whom Beatport has called [the face of the current wave of UK garage in the US](https://www.beatportal.com/articles/1052209-artist-of-the-month-sammy-virji). His [DJ Mag HQ set went viral](https://djmag.com/features/sammy-virji-global-garage-phenomenon-interview), and a run of North American headline tours followed. He's modest about it. Talking to Beatport about leading garage's global revival, he played down his own part: ["I'm just at the right place at the right time."](https://www.beatportal.com/articles/249663-sammy-virji-on-leading-uk-garages-global-revolution-i-was-just-in-the-right-place-at-the-right-time)

## MPH, Chris Lake and the house connection

If Virji is the face of the revival, MPH is a good example of how it actually travelled. MPH is Myles Fairbairn, a producer from Canterbury, England, whom [Leeds Festival describes](https://www.leedsfestival.com/artists/mph) as one of the driving forces in modern UK garage and bassline. He started as a DJ, and told [Beatport](https://www.beatportal.com/articles/201881-mph-flava-d-has-been-the-biggest-influence-on-my-sound) that he used to watch DJ EZ's Boiler Room sets religiously, studying how he blended each tune, while the producer Flava D shaped his sound more than anyone.

Here's where the history loops. According to [his Qobuz biography](https://www.qobuz.com/gb-en/interpreter/mph/1695251), Fairbairn traced garage's roots back to Todd Edwards, the American producer whose chopped vocals helped make the British sound in the first place. The new wave learned from the old wave, which learned from New Jersey.

MPH built his catalogue on UK labels, including [DJ EZ's Nuvolve, Kiwi Rekords and Crucast](https://www.beatportal.com/articles/201881-mph-flava-d-has-been-the-biggest-influence-on-my-sound), before landing on Night Bass, the label run by AC Slater, for his albums *132.00FM* and *Refraction*. By 2025, [his biography notes](https://www.qobuz.com/gb-en/interpreter/mph/1695251), he had played Coachella. Then came a change of home that says a lot about where garage is heading: Black Book Records, the label Chris Lake launched in 2017.

Chris Lake is an English [house and tech house producer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chris_Lake) whose label has released records by [Green Velvet, Armand Van Helden and Kevin Saunderson](https://www.axs.com/se/artists/775729), while also working as a launchpad for newer names. According to [Black Book's announcement of "Flex It"](https://www.beatportal.com/articles/1295810-mph-and-ae-join-forces-on-flex-it-out-now-on-black-book-records), MPH's Black Book debut, "Raw", reached number one on Beatport's UK garage chart, and his collaboration with Lake, "Reach For You" featuring Kelly Lee Owens, appeared on Lake's album *Chemistry*. "Flex It" itself, released in 2026, pairs MPH with the LA-based producer and songwriter Æ. Thomas Bangalter of Daft Punk played it on a livestream alongside Fred again.. in Paris, and Lake dropped it at a sold-out show in San Diego. It went on to reach [number seven on Beatport's overall chart](https://breakingandentering.net/2026/05/02/mph-unconditional-new-single-breakaway-arizona/) and number one on its breakbeat chart.

Lake's links run through Virji too. The two have released two collaborations, ["Summertime Blues" and "925"](https://www.beatportal.com/articles/1047369-sammy-virji-and-chris-lake-meet-again-for-propulsive-single-925), and this September Virji teamed up with Barry Can't Swim on ["I Could Be Madonna"](https://routenote.com/radar/?p=4289), a direct homage to classic UK garage.

That's the real story behind this week's Vancouver lineup. A garage producer from Canterbury is signed to an English house producer's label, collaborating with a vocalist from Los Angeles, and being played by a French house legend. Garage and house, which started as the same music in New York and New Jersey, are running back into each other. Not everyone loves it. KEYMAG has asked whether the new [tech house and garage crossover](https://www.keymag.co.uk/nukg-monthly/tag/rinse+fm) is a faithful callback or a move towards commercialism, and it's a fair question. But it's hard to argue the sound hasn't come full circle.

## The arena problem

Big shows prove there's an audience, but they don't prove there's a scene. A night at the Convention Centre or the PNE Forum happens once; a scene is what happens on the other fifty weekends of the year, in smaller rooms, with local DJs, local producers and the crews who set those rooms up.

The original UK garage scene was built on pirate radio stations, small clubs and record shops, run by people whose names rarely made it onto a flyer. If North America wants more than a string of tour stops, it needs those things too: local nights that run between big shows, local producers making their own version of the sound, and the lighting, sound and door crews who make a Tuesday feel like an event.

That's the part we want to document. When Andy Lewis was being teased in Miami, the funny sped-up garage was already a whole culture in London, built by people nobody outside it knew. Chris Lake and MPH play the Convention Centre on Halloween, and Sammy Virji's doors open at the PNE Forum at 6:30 on November 6. The question for Vancouver, and every city on these tours, is what's still open the week after.
