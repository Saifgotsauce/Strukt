Most people who make visuals for clubs and festivals started by teaching themselves free software, playing visuals at small nights for little or no money, and building a reel of their own content. The ones who get hired for big shows also learn the unglamorous part: how screens, media servers and lighting desks talk to each other. Here's the route, and the people who built it.

## What the job actually is

"Visuals" covers a few different jobs that often get lumped together:

- **The VJ** mixes video live, the way a DJ mixes records, reacting to the music in the room.
- **The content designer** makes the animations, loops and films that get played.
- **The screens or media server technician** makes sure all of that reaches the LED walls and projectors, in sync and in focus.
- **The creative director** decides what the whole show should look and feel like.

On a big tour, those are separate people. For Fred again..'s run of shows in Europe and North America in the fall of 2023, the technical screens company Visual Endeavors published [a case study naming the key players](https://visualendeavors.com/fred-again/): **Lucy Hickling** as creative director, **Matt Pittman** as production and lighting designer, **Grant Draper** as tour director and **Dermot Lynch** as production manager. The show was unusual. Samples played live triggered video clips, and editors changed small parts of the content each night as the music changed, so no two nights looked the same.

*That case study doesn't name the editors who rebuilt the content night after night, or Visual Endeavors' own team on the tour. We couldn't find them anywhere else either.*

In a club, one person often does all of it: makes the content, runs it live and fixes the projector when it overheats at 2 a.m.

## Learn the tools for free

The two programs you'll meet most often both let you learn without paying.

**TouchDesigner.** It's made by Derivative, a company in Toronto [founded in 2000 by **Greg Hermanovic**, **Rob Bairos** and **Jarrett Smith**](https://derivative.ca/UserGuide/TouchDesigner). Hermanovic also shares an Academy Award for his earlier work on visual effects software, and in the mid-1990s he [moonlighted as an audiovisual artist at Toronto's early raves](https://ableton.com/en/blog/visualizing-sound-a-beginners-guide-to-using-touchdesigner-with-live). A chance meeting with Richie Hawtin later led to their first audiovisual collaboration, at the Mutek festival in Montreal, and TouchDesigner's user base grew quickly after that. The [Non-Commercial version is free](https://derivative.ca/product/touchdesigner-non-commercial/77) for personal use and learning, with output limited to 1280 by 1280 pixels. The catch is in the licence: you can't use it for any work you're paid for, so you'll need a commercial licence once the gigs start paying.

**Resolume.** It was created by **Edwin de Koning** and **Bart van der Ploeg** in the Netherlands, who, by their [own account](https://resolume.com/company), started programming it in 1998 because they wanted to VJ and couldn't find software that did what they needed. They quickly decided their software was better than their VJing, and have worked on it full time since 2002. You can use Resolume without a licence to learn, but [everything you output carries a visual and audio watermark](https://bookstack.hku.nl/books/resolume/page/resolume-introduction) until you buy one.

TouchDesigner has the steeper learning curve and does far more; Resolume is faster to start performing with. Many working VJs end up using both.

**A practice routine that actually helps.** Pick a track you know well. Make or collect three short loops, one for the intro, one for the build and one for the drop, using your own footage or properly licensed loops. Map them to a cheap MIDI controller so you can switch, fade and add one effect by hand. Then perform the track twenty times until the drop lands without you looking at the screen. Record a run, watch it back with the sound off, and ask whether the visuals still feel like the song.

## How people actually got their start

There's no single route, but the stories rhyme.

- **Steve McCorry**, who works as Glass Crane, is a [self-taught motion designer from Rochester, New York](https://consciouselectronic.com/2020/11/18/edm-industry-top-ten-visual-artists/) who has made visuals for Tipper and Excision.
- **Ethan Stewart**, who works as tenstriip, got his start in digital design at CU Denver and worked his way up to a residency at The Black Box, a Colorado music venue.
- The VJ **Zebbler** turned performing into teaching. In 2017 he [joined Berklee's campus in Valencia, Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zebbler) as an instructor in video production and VJ and video mapping.
- **Benton C Bainbridge** was doing live visuals long before the word VJ was common, and went on to [VJ for the Beastie Boys' 2004 MTV VMA performance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benton_C_Bainbridge).

The common thread is a residency: a regular night where someone let them play visuals every week. That's where you build a reel, make your mistakes and get noticed.

## The jobs that take beginners

**A residency at a small venue.** Plenty of clubs and bars have a projector or an LED wall and nobody running it. Offer to do visuals for a regular night. It's rarely well paid at first, but it gives you weekly practice in front of a real crowd.

**Content work for other VJs and designers.** Bigger shows need huge amounts of content. Making loops and edits for an established designer teaches you what works on a big screen.

**LED and projection crew.** Rental companies and production crews need people to build LED walls, hang projectors and run cables. You'll learn how the hardware works, which is what makes a designer employable on tour.

**Your own collective's nights.** Teaming up with local DJs and promoters is how most of the people above started.

Whatever the job, the same habits get you rehired: arrive early to test outputs, carry spare cables and adapters, keep backups of every file on a second drive, and never leave the screens black.

## Use content you have the rights to

The fastest way to lose a gig is to project something you don't have the rights to. Clips from films, music videos or other artists' work can get a night shut down or a venue in trouble. Shoot your own footage, generate your own animation, or use loops that are clearly licensed for performance, and keep a record of where each one came from.

## Quick answers

**Do I need a degree to become a VJ?**
No. Most VJs and visual designers taught themselves with free or trial software and learned by doing regular nights. Art or design training helps with content, but crews hire on reels and reliability.

**Which software should I learn first, TouchDesigner or Resolume?**
Resolume is quicker to start performing with, and TouchDesigner does more but has a steeper learning curve. You can learn both free: TouchDesigner's Non-Commercial version is limited to 1280 by 1280 output and can't be used for paid work, and Resolume adds a watermark until you buy a licence.

**Can I use TouchDesigner's free version for paid gigs?**
No. The Non-Commercial licence is for personal, educational and non-paying use only. Paid work needs a commercial licence.

**How do I get my first VJ gig?**
Offer to run visuals for a regular night at a small venue, team up with local DJs and promoters, and build a reel from those shows.
