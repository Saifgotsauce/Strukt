Almost nobody gets into lighting the way careers advice says they should. Most people in the booth started by carrying cases, coiling cables or saying yes to a small gig nobody else wanted. This guide covers the route that actually works.

## Start where the shows are small

Student parties, small club nights, community events and local bands all need someone who will show up early and stay late. These rooms are where you learn the basics without a huge rig to break: how fixtures are hung, how power is run, and how a night feels from behind the desk instead of in front of it.

Offer to help load in. Watch whoever is running lights. Ask questions after the show, never during it.

## Learn a console before you touch one

The big lighting consoles have free software versions you can run on a laptop, so you can learn to program at home. Build looks, save cues and practise timing them to tracks you know well. When someone finally lets you near a real desk, you will already know where things are.

## Safety is the job, not a side note

Lights hang over people's heads. Learn how fixtures are secured, why safety cables matter, and when to say no to working at height without the right training. The people who get hired again are the ones nobody has to worry about.

## Build a portfolio from night one

Film short clips of every show you work on, even small ones. Note what you did: programmed the opener, rigged the side truss, ran the desk for the support set. A folder of real work beats any résumé line.

## Get credited

This is the part most people skip. When you work a night, make sure the promoter knows your name and tags you. Credits are how the next promoter finds you.
