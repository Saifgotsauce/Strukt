Most people who run lights for clubs and festivals got there without a degree. They learned a lighting console at home on free software, took the jobs nobody else wanted around shows, and were careful enough with heavy things over people's heads that crews kept calling them back. That's the whole route. The rest of this guide is the detail.

## What the job actually is

On paper, a lighting technician sets up, operates and packs down the lights for a show. The US occupational database [O*NET](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/27-4015.00) lists the day-to-day: loading and positioning fixtures, programming consoles, matching brightness and colour to the designer's plan, adding colour filters and gobos (the stencils that shape a beam), testing everything, and flagging broken gear before it fails mid-show.

In practice, at a club night or a festival stage, the work splits in two. There's the physical half, which is carrying, hanging, cabling and focusing fixtures, often from early morning. Then there's the programming half, which is building the looks and cues that make the room react when the music does. Nearly everyone starts on the physical half. The programming half is the part you can teach yourself before anyone lets you near a desk.

## Learn to program before anyone hands you a console

You don't need to buy a console to learn one. The two systems you'll meet most on club and festival stages both have free versions that run on a laptop.

**grandMA3 onPC.** MA Lighting offers the [grandMA3 onPC software](https://www.malighting.com/special/grandma3-onpc/) as a free download for Windows and Mac, and it's the same software that runs on their full-size consoles. They also run a [free e-learning course](https://www.malighting.com/ma-university/first-steps/) with a starter show built in, so you have something to program from day one.

**ChamSys MagicQ.** [MagicQ PC](https://bookstack.hku.nl/books/controlling-lights/page/magicq-lighting-software-mimicking-a-light-console/revisions/951) is free on Windows, Mac and Linux, with 64 universes of output enabled over network protocols like Art-Net and sACN, and it includes a 3D visualiser, so you can watch your programming on virtual fixtures. ChamSys hosts [video tutorials](https://chamsyslighting.com/pages/be-home-page) for it too.

Pick one and stay with it for a month before trying the other. Concepts carry over between consoles; muscle memory doesn't.

**A practice routine that actually helps.** Choose a track you know well, ideally something with a long build and a clean drop. In the visualiser, patch a simple rig: eight moving heads on a truss, four wash lights on the floor and a strobe. Then build three cues: a moody look for the build, a held breath right before the drop (everything dark except one beam), and a full-room hit for the drop. Run them live against the track until you can land the drop without looking at your hands. Then do it again with a track you've never heard. That second part is what nights are actually like.

## The jobs that take beginners

Nobody hires a first-timer to program a festival stage. Here's where people actually get in.

**Load-in and load-out crew.** Every show needs hands to unload trucks, carry cases, build truss and pack it all away afterwards. Local crewing companies and venues hire for this constantly. It's hard, early and late, but you'll see how a rig goes together, and you'll meet the people who decide who gets called next time.

**Warehouse prep at a rental company.** The companies that rent out lighting need people to test fixtures, build and label cables and pack gear for upcoming shows. It's indoor work during the day, and it teaches you every piece of equipment you'll later hang.

**Small rooms that run themselves.** Student nights, small clubs and DIY parties often have a basic rig and nobody to run it. Offering to operate is how many programmers got their first real hours on a desk, along with their first mistakes in front of a crowd small enough to forgive them.

**Followspot.** Bigger venues and festivals hire operators for followspots, the large manually aimed lights that track a performer. It's a common first step into the show itself rather than the setup around it.

Whatever the job, the habits that get you rehired are the same: arrive before call time, bring your own gloves and a small torch, ask what needs doing instead of waiting to be told, and never touch the desk unless someone tells you to.

## Safety is the part that gets you called back

Lights hang over the heads of performers and crowds. Most of what makes a technician trusted is boring and invisible: every fixture secured with a safety cable, nobody standing under a load as it's flown, power run tidily and rated properly, and saying no when you're asked to work at height without the right training or equipment.

Later on, there are formal certifications that prove this. In North America, the Entertainment Technician Certification Program, known as ETCP, certifies [entertainment electricians](https://etcp.esta.org/certify/certify_electrical.html) and [riggers for arenas and theatres](https://etcp.esta.org/certify/certify_rigger.html). They aren't beginner qualifications. Candidates need 30 eligibility points, earned mostly through documented work experience, and riggers must be at least 21. Treat them as a goal for year three or four, and start logging your hours now so you can prove them when you get there.

## What it pays

Pay varies hugely by country, city and whether you're on staff or freelance, so be wary of any single number. For a reference point, O*NET's US figures put the [median wage for lighting technicians](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/27-4015.00) at $29.11 an hour, or $60,560 a year, based on 2024 data. That covers all lighting technicians, including film and television, not only live events.

Early freelance work is usually paid by the day or the call, and the first year often means more hours than money. The jump comes when people start booking you for programming and operating rather than labour.

## Build a portfolio from your first night

Film fifteen to thirty seconds of every show you work on, even the small ones, and write down exactly what you did: rigged the side truss, programmed the opener, ran the desk for the support act. Keep it all in one place with dates and venues. When someone asks what you can do, a folder of short clips with honest notes beats any résumé line.

And make sure your name goes with the work. Ask the promoter to tag you when they post. Credits are how the next promoter finds you.

## Quick answers

**Do I need a degree to become a lighting technician?**
No. Most club and festival techs learned through hands-on work. A degree in technical theatre can help, and it counts towards some certifications later, but crews hire on experience and reliability.

**Which lighting console should I learn first?**
Download grandMA3 onPC or ChamSys MagicQ, since both are free and both appear on club and festival stages. Pick one, learn it properly, then try the other.

**How long does it take to start running shows?**
It depends on how many hours you get. People who work load-ins regularly and practise programming at home often operate small rooms within their first year. Programming big stages usually takes several years.

**Do I need certification to work in lighting?**
Not to start. Certifications such as ETCP in North America are aimed at experienced electricians and riggers and require documented work hours before you can sit the exam.
